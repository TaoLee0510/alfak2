\begin{abstract}
\texttt{alfak2} estimates fitness landscapes of aneuploid karyotypes from two
sparse single-cell samples of a cell population. Its default model, the sparse
joint count model, describes both samples with a single count likelihood
defined on the abundant karyotypes and all of their one-step neighbours,
whether observed or not, and integrates cell division and chromosome
missegregation in closed form. Fitness is inferred from changes in clone
frequency, from the abundance at which mutation--selection balance maintains
rare karyotypes, and from the absence of neighbouring karyotypes; karyotypes
beyond the model domain are predicted from a fitted trend. This document
specifies the model (Section~\ref{sec:sparse-joint}), relates it to
\texttt{alfakR} (Section~\ref{sec:alfakR}), describes the default analysis
workflow (Section~\ref{sec:workflow}) and summarises its validation
(Section~\ref{sec:validation}). The earlier two-layer pipeline, its nine
second-layer extrapolation methods and the joint-count pipeline are described
in the Supplement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{Data}

A cell population is sampled at two time points, $0$ and $T$. Each sampled
cell is assigned a karyotype, represented as a copy-number vector
$x=(x_1,\ldots,x_p)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^p$ over $p$ chromosomes ($p=22$ for
the human autosomes). The observations are the counts
\[
  y_{0v},\ y_{1v}\qquad (v\in\mathcal{O}),
  \qquad N_0=\sum_v y_{0v},\quad N_1=\sum_v y_{1v},
\]
of the observed karyotypes $\mathcal{O}$ in the two samples. Typical samples
comprise several hundred to several thousand cells. Most observed karyotypes
are represented by one or two cells, and most karyotypes that differ from an
abundant clone by a single copy-number change, which are the principal targets
of inference, are not observed at all. The method is designed for this
setting of two time points and sparse counts.

\subsection{Population Dynamics}

Cells of karyotype $i$ divide at birth rate $b_i$. At each division, a
daughter cell inherits karyotype $j$ with probability $P_{ij}$, which is
determined by chromosome missegregation (Section~\ref{sec:sj-division});
daughters with a copy number outside $[c_{\min},c_{\max}]$ are inviable. The
expected abundances $n(t)$ then satisfy the linear system
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dn_j}{dt}=\bigl(2P_{jj}-1\bigr)b_j\,n_j+\sum_{i\ne j}2P_{ij}\,b_i\,n_i .
  \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
The fitness of karyotype $i$ is defined as its net growth rate,
\begin{equation}
  d_i=(2P_{ii}-1)\,b_i ,
  \label{eq:net}
\end{equation}
and $M_{ji}=2P_{ij}b_i$ ($j\ne i$) denotes the mutational flux from $i$ to
$j$, so that $dn/dt=(\operatorname{diag}(d)+M)\,n$.

\subsection{Identifiability}

Each sample is a draw from the composition of the population rather than from
its absolute size. Consequently, two samples identify differences in fitness
but not its absolute level: adding a constant to every $d_i$ alters the
population size but leaves its composition unchanged. Fitness is therefore
reported relative to the major karyotypes (Section~\ref{sec:sj-relative}).
The birth rate enters the dynamics separately, through the mutational flux,
and is specified rather than estimated (Section~\ref{sec:sj-rate}).

\section{The Sparse Joint Count Model}
\label{sec:sparse-joint}

\subsection{Model Rationale}
\label{sec:sj-design}

Two cross-sectional samples provide three sources of information about
fitness.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Frequency change of abundant clones.} A clone observed in many
cells in both samples changes its frequency by a factor
$\exp\{(d_i-\bar d)T\}$, which determines its fitness relative to the other
clones.

\item \textbf{Abundance of rare karyotypes.} A karyotype $j$ that differs from
an abundant clone $p$ by a single missegregation is replenished at each
division of $p$ and depleted by selection at rate $d_p-d_j$. Its abundance
approaches
\begin{equation}
  \frac{n_j}{n_p}\approx\frac{M_{jp}}{d_p-d_j},
  \label{eq:balance-limit}
\end{equation}
the mutation--selection balance. At balance, the frequency of $j$ tracks that
of $p$, so that its change between the samples is uninformative about $d_j$,
whereas its level is informative.

\item \textbf{Absence of neighbouring karyotypes.} A neighbour of an abundant
clone that is not observed in a sample of several hundred cells has an
abundance, and hence a fitness deficit, that is constrained by
(\ref{eq:balance-limit}). Zero counts therefore constitute evidence.
\end{enumerate}

The model is constructed to use all three sources. A single count likelihood
covers both samples, so that each zero count is weighted according to the size
of its sample. The domain includes every one-step neighbour of an abundant
clone, whether observed or not, so that absences enter the likelihood.
Mutational influx is integrated over the entire interval rather than applied
once after growth, which links the abundance of a rare karyotype to its
fitness. The domain is restricted to the abundant clones and their
neighbours, which keeps the Hessian of the latent variables sparse for
domains of several thousand states. Fitness is reported relative to the
abundant clones, which is the quantity that two samples identify.

\paragraph{Terminology.}
The model is sparse in two respects. It is designed for sparse data: two
samples of a few hundred to a few thousand cells in a karyotype space in which
each clone has $2p=44$ one-step and $2p^2=968$ two-step neighbours, so that
most karyotypes whose fitness is sought are not observed, even when the
observed karyotypes themselves are well sampled. Its structure is sparse as
well: the likelihood is restricted to the major karyotypes and their one-step
neighbours, and the first-order dynamics couple each state only to its
neighbours, so that the Hessian of the latent variables is sparse. The model
is joint in that a single likelihood covers both samples and all states, and
it models counts rather than frequencies.

\subsection{Major Karyotypes and Model Domain}
\label{sec:sj-domain}

The share of karyotype $v$ is its larger frequency over the two samples,
\begin{equation}
  \phi_v=\max\Bigl(\frac{y_{0v}}{N_0},\frac{y_{1v}}{N_1}\Bigr).
  \label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
A karyotype is classified as \emph{major} if
\begin{equation}
  \phi_v\ge\phi_{\min}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{0v}+y_{1v}\ge c_{\min},
  \qquad \phi_{\min}=0.01,\ c_{\min}=3 .
  \label{eq:major}
\end{equation}
The share criterion adapts the classification to the sample size, since a
single cell in a sample of 3000 may belong to the mutational cloud of a clone.
The count criterion is also required: if a single cell sufficed, every cell of
a small sample would lie on a major karyotype, and every unobserved neighbour
would appear strongly deleterious. If no karyotype satisfies
(\ref{eq:major}), the most abundant karyotype is designated major. The model
domain is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D}=\mathcal{M}\cup\bigl\{x_m\pm e_c:\ m\in\mathcal{M},\
  c=1,\ldots,p,\ c_{\min}\le(x_m\pm e_c)_c\le c_{\max}\bigr\},
  \label{eq:domain}
\end{equation}
where $e_c$ is the unit vector of chromosome $c$, $c_{\min}=1$, and
$c_{\max}$ defaults to one above the largest observed copy number. If
$|\mathcal{D}|$ would exceed an upper bound (5000 states), $\phi_{\min}$ is
raised to the smallest share for which the bound holds.

Observed karyotypes outside $\mathcal{D}$ are excluded from the likelihood
together with their cells. Such a karyotype would otherwise be included only
because it was observed, whereas its unobserved counterparts at the same
distance would not, and its count would then be attributed to high fitness.
It is instead predicted at the mean level of the observed karyotypes within
the domain (Section~\ref{sec:sj-predict}).

\subsection{Missegregation Kernel}
\label{sec:sj-division}

For a chromosome with $i$ copies, of which $m$ missegregate (each with
probability $\beta$) and are allocated at random to the two daughters, a
daughter receives $j$ copies with probability
\begin{equation}
  p_\beta(i\to j)=\sum_{\substack{m=|i-j|\\ m\equiv|i-j|\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}}^{i}
  \binom{i}{m}\beta^m(1-\beta)^{i-m}\,2^{-m}\binom{m}{(m+i-j)/2}.
  \label{eq:pij}
\end{equation}
Chromosomes segregate independently; hence, for $x_j=x_i\pm e_c$,
\begin{equation}
  P_{ij}=p_\beta\bigl(x_{ic}\to x_{ic}\pm1\bigr)\prod_{c'\ne c}p_\beta(x_{ic'}\to x_{ic'}),
  \qquad
  P_{ii}=\prod_{c}p_\beta(x_{ic}\to x_{ic}).
  \label{eq:Pij}
\end{equation}
Only one-step transitions are modelled. For $\beta$ of order $10^{-4}$ to
$10^{-3}$ per copy, transitions involving two or more copies in a single
division are smaller by a further factor of order $p\beta$.

\subsection{Approximate Solution of the Population Dynamics}
\label{sec:sj-forward}

System (\ref{eq:ode}) is solved with $\exp(\operatorname{diag}(d)t)$ treated
exactly and $M$ to first order. By variation of constants,
$n(T)=e^{DT}n(0)+\int_0^Te^{D(T-s)}Mn(s)\,ds$ with $D=\operatorname{diag}(d)$.
Replacing $n(s)$ in the integrand by $e^{Ds}n(0)$ yields
\begin{equation}
  n_j(T)=e^{d_jT}n_j(0)+\sum_{i\sim j}M_{ji}\,\varphi_1(d_j,d_i;T)\,n_i(0),
  \qquad
  \varphi_1(a,b;T)=\int_0^Te^{a(T-s)+bs}\,ds=\frac{e^{aT}-e^{bT}}{a-b},
  \label{eq:forward}
\end{equation}
with $\varphi_1(a,a;T)=Te^{aT}$. The neglected terms are of second order in
$M$; they correspond to cells that arise two or more missegregations away
within the interval, or that revert. Because each state depends only on itself
and its one-step neighbours, the Hessian of the latent variables is sparse.

\subsection{Observation Model}
\label{sec:sj-obs}

With $n_i(0)=e^{\zeta_i}$ the expected count in the first sample, the counts
are modelled as
\begin{equation}
  y_{0i}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}\bigl(e^{\zeta_i}\bigr),
  \qquad
  y_{1i}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}\bigl(\lambda_{1i}\bigr),
  \quad
  \lambda_{1i}=\exp\bigl\{\theta_1-d_rT\bigr\}\,n_i(T),
  \qquad i\in\mathcal{D},
  \label{eq:poisson}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the most abundant karyotype and $\theta_1$ absorbs the ratio of
sample sizes and the common growth of the population. Conditional on the two
sample totals, independent Poisson counts are multinomially distributed with
probabilities proportional to the intensities; (\ref{eq:poisson}) is therefore
the Poisson representation of two multinomial samples. It is preferred
because a multinomial normalisation over the domain would couple every pair
of states. The likelihood is formulated in counts rather than frequencies:
frequencies of $1/500$ and $10/5000$ are equal, but the latter carries ten
times the information, and a zero count among 3000 cells is more informative
than one among 50. For held-out refits (Section~\ref{sec:sj-alfakR}), the two
terms of each state are multiplied by a weight $w_i\in\{0,1\}$; in a standard
fit, all weights equal one.

\subsection{Prior on Initial Abundances}
\label{sec:sj-initial}

\paragraph{Independent initial abundances.}
With \texttt{initial\_state = "free"}, each $\zeta_i$ has an independent
prior,
\begin{equation}
  \zeta_i\sim
  \begin{cases}
  \Normal\bigl(\log y_{0i},\,10^2\bigr), & y_{0i}>0,\\
  \Normal\bigl(\log(y_{1i}N_0/N_1),\,\sigma_0^2\bigr), & y_{0i}=0<y_{1i},\\
  \Normal\bigl(\log N_0+\log f_0,\,\sigma_0^2\bigr), & y_{0i}=y_{1i}=0,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:free-prior}
\end{equation}
with $f_0=10^{-5}$ and $\sigma_0=3$. The prior is essentially uninformative
for karyotypes observed in the first sample, centred on the later frequency
for karyotypes observed only in the second sample, and concentrated at low
frequency for karyotypes not observed. It is specified on the frequency scale
rather than the count scale, because a prior on counts would assign the
several thousand unobserved karyotypes of a domain a substantial share of a
small sample.

\paragraph{Mutation--selection balance (default).}
With \texttt{initial\_state = "balance"}, the major karyotypes retain the
prior (\ref{eq:free-prior}), and each non-major state $j$ is centred on the
abundance that its major neighbours would have generated over a history of
length $h$, assuming constant growth at their current rates. Integrating
(\ref{eq:ode}) for $j$ from $n_j(-h)=0$, with $n_p(s)=n_p(0)e^{d_ps}$ for
$s\in[-h,0]$, gives
\begin{equation}
  n_j(0)=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{M},\,p\sim j}M_{jp}\,n_p(0)\,e^{-d_ph}\,\varphi_1(d_j,d_p;h),
  \label{eq:balance-start}
\end{equation}
and the prior
\begin{equation}
  \zeta_j\sim\Normal\Bigl(\log\!\!\sum_{p\in\mathcal{M},\,p\sim j}\!\!M_{jp}\,
  e^{\zeta_p-d_ph}\,\varphi_1(d_j,d_p;h),\ \sigma_\zeta^2\Bigr),
  \qquad j\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{M}.
  \label{eq:balance-prior}
\end{equation}
For a long history and a less fit neighbour,
$e^{-d_ph}\varphi_1(d_j,d_p;h)\to1/(d_p-d_j)$, and (\ref{eq:balance-start})
reduces to (\ref{eq:balance-limit}). The count of $j$ in the first sample
thereby also informs $d_j$. The defaults are $h=T$ and $\sigma_\zeta=1$, which
permits departures from balance by a factor of approximately $e$; results
were insensitive to this choice (values of 0.3, 0.5 and 1 yielded errors
within 0.003 of one another).

This prior assumes that fitness before the first sample was similar to
fitness between the samples. In simulations in which the landscape changed at
the first sample, as under the onset of treatment, it nevertheless remained
more accurate than independent initial abundances. When the samples were
separated by approximately 30 divisions it was less accurate, because the
default $h=T$ is then too short for the mutational cloud to have formed; a
longer, explicitly specified $h$ addresses this case.

\subsection{Prior on the Fitness Landscape}
\label{sec:sj-prior}

Log birth rates follow a trend with an independent residual,
\begin{equation}
  g_i\sim\Normal\bigl(z_i^\top\gamma,\ \sigma_r^2\bigr),\qquad i\in\mathcal{D},
  \label{eq:trend}
\end{equation}
with covariates
\begin{equation}
  z_i=\Bigl(1,\ (x_i-\bar x)^\top,\ \bigl((x_i-\bar x)^{\circ2}\bigr)^\top,\
  \operatorname{dist}(x_i,\mathcal{M})^{0.85}\Bigr)^\top,
  \qquad
  \bar x=\frac{\sum_v(y_{0v}+y_{1v})x_v}{\sum_v(y_{0v}+y_{1v})},
  \label{eq:design}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathcal{M})=\min_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\|x-x_m\|_1$
and $^{\circ2}$ denotes the elementwise square. The covariates comprise an
intercept, a dosage effect and a curvature term for each chromosome relative
to the count-weighted mean karyotype, and a distance term reflecting the
slightly sublinear decline of mean fitness with distance from the major
karyotypes. The distance is measured to major karyotypes only; if it were
measured to all observed karyotypes, the covariate of a neighbour would depend
on its own count. The coefficients have priors
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_0\sim\Normal(\log b_0,\ 0.05^2),\quad
  \gamma_{\mathrm{dos}}\sim\Normal(0,\sigma_d^2),\quad
  \gamma_{\mathrm{curv}}\sim\Normal(0,\sigma_c^2),\quad
  \gamma_{\mathrm{dist}}\sim\Normal(0,0.5^2),
  \label{eq:coef-prior}
\end{equation}
where $b_0$ is the specified baseline birth rate, and the scales have priors
\begin{equation}
  \log\sigma_k\sim\Normal\bigl(\log 0.01,\ 1.5^2\bigr),\qquad k\in\{d,c,r\},
  \label{eq:scale-prior}
\end{equation}
centred on an effect of 1\% per copy-number step and sufficiently wide to
accommodate effects between 0.05\% and 20\%.

\subsection{Posterior Inference}
\label{sec:sj-inference}

The latent variables are $u=(\zeta,g,\gamma,\theta_1)$ and the
hyperparameters are $\psi=(\log\sigma_d,\log\sigma_c,\log\sigma_r)$. The
negative log joint density $\ell(u,\psi)$ combines (\ref{eq:poisson}),
(\ref{eq:free-prior}) or (\ref{eq:balance-prior}), and
(\ref{eq:trend})--(\ref{eq:scale-prior}). The hyperparameters are estimated
by maximising the Laplace approximation to the marginal likelihood,
\begin{equation}
  \hat\psi=\arg\min_{\psi\in[\log10^{-4},\log0.5]^3}
  \Bigl\{\ell\bigl(\hat u(\psi),\psi\bigr)+\tfrac12\log\det H(\psi)\Bigr\},
  \qquad
  \hat u(\psi)=\arg\min_u\ell(u,\psi),
  \label{eq:laplace}
\end{equation}
where $H(\psi)=\nabla_u^2\ell$ evaluated at $\hat u(\psi)$. The inner
minimisation uses Newton's method and the outer minimisation a bounded
quasi-Newton method. The bounds are imposed because the inner problem may
become degenerate outside them. If the outer optimisation fails, the
prior-centred values of $\psi$ are used and the failure is recorded.

Conditional on $\hat\psi$, the posterior of $u$ is approximated by a Gaussian
distribution centred at $\hat u$ with covariance $\bigl(H_E\bigr)^{-1}$,
where $H_E$ is the \emph{expected} information: the Hessian of $\ell$ with the
counts replaced by their fitted expectations, $y_{0i}\to e^{\hat\zeta_i}$ and
$y_{1i}\to\hat\lambda_{1i}$, and with the curvature of the balance prior
evaluated at zero residual. Because the intensities are not log-linear in $g$,
the observed Hessian can have directions of negative curvature where the
counts are poorly fitted, in which case its inverse yields undefined or
implausibly large standard deviations. The matrix $H_E$ is positive definite
by construction, its likelihood component being a Gauss--Newton matrix. It is
factorised once by sparse Cholesky decomposition, $H_E=P^\top LL^\top P$,
from which variances, covariances and posterior draws are obtained
(Section~\ref{sec:sj-alfakR}).

\subsection{Relative Fitness and Posterior Uncertainty}
\label{sec:sj-relative}

The fitness of a domain state is $d_i=(2P_{ii}-1)e^{g_i}$. It is reported
relative to the count-weighted mean fitness of the major karyotypes,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_i=d_i-R,\qquad R=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}w_md_m,\qquad
  w_m=\frac{y_{0m}+y_{1m}}{\sum_{m'\in\mathcal{M}}(y_{0m'}+y_{1m'})}.
  \label{eq:relative}
\end{equation}
By the delta method, with $\Sigma=H_E^{-1}$ and $a$ the vector with entries
$w_md_m$ at the positions of $g_m$,
\begin{equation}
  s_i^2=\operatorname{Var}(\rho_i)\approx d_i^2\Sigma_{g_ig_i}+a^\top\Sigma a-2d_i(\Sigma a)_{g_i}.
  \label{eq:relative-var}
\end{equation}
A shift common to all log birth rates, through which the uncertainty of the
baseline enters, changes $d_i$ and $R$ equally to first order and therefore
cancels in $\rho_i$.

\subsection{Calibration of Predictive Uncertainty}
\label{sec:sj-calibration}

The posterior standard deviation $s_i$ is conditional on $\hat\psi$ and
assumes that the model is correctly specified: the dynamics are first order in
the flux, and the dispersion of unobserved karyotypes is inferred from
observed karyotypes that selection has rendered similar. In simulations, its
nominal 95\% intervals covered approximately three quarters of observed
karyotypes and one half of unobserved ones. The reported standard deviation
therefore includes two model-error terms,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde s_i^2=s_i^2+(\kappa\rho_i)^2+\bigl(a\,b_0\,(1+\operatorname{dist}(x_i,\mathcal{M}))\bigr)^2,
  \qquad \kappa=0.6,\ a=0.009.
  \label{eq:calibration}
\end{equation}
The first term makes the uncertainty proportional to the predicted distance
from the reference; the second represents an irreducible error per division
that increases with distance from the major karyotypes. The constants were
estimated by maximising the Gaussian likelihood of the prediction errors
$\rho_i^{\mathrm{true}}-\rho_i$ on the development landscapes of the
benchmark; an additional multiplicative factor on $s_i$ was estimated to be
close to zero and omitted. On landscapes not used for estimation, the nominal
95\% intervals covered between 89\% and 100\% of true values, depending on
wavelength and distance (Section~\ref{sec:validation}). Both $\tilde s_i$ and
$s_i$ are returned.

\subsection{Prediction Beyond the Model Domain}
\label{sec:sj-predict}

A karyotype $x$ outside $\mathcal{D}$ is predicted from the trend,
\begin{equation}
  \hat g(x)=z(x)^\top\hat\gamma,\qquad
  \operatorname{Var}\hat g(x)=z(x)^\top\Sigma_{\gamma\gamma}z(x)+\hat\sigma_r^2,
  \label{eq:predict-trend}
\end{equation}
with $\hat d(x)=(2P_{xx}-1)e^{\hat g(x)}$; relative fitness and its variance
follow from (\ref{eq:relative})--(\ref{eq:relative-var}), using the covariance
between $\gamma$ and the reference. An observed karyotype excluded from the
domain is predicted at the mean level of the observed non-major karyotypes
within it, $\mathcal{L}=\{i\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{M}:y_{0i}+y_{1i}>0\}$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat g=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{L}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}}\hat g_i,\qquad
  \operatorname{Var}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{L}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}}\operatorname{Var}(g_i)
  +\operatorname{var}_{i\in\mathcal{L}}(\hat g_i),
  \label{eq:predict-level}
\end{equation}
because an observed karyotype is considerably closer in fitness to the major
karyotypes than an unobserved karyotype at the same distance. The calibration
(\ref{eq:calibration}) applies in both cases.

\subsection{Specification of the Baseline Birth Rate}
\label{sec:sj-rate}

The birth rate enters (\ref{eq:ode}) through $M$, independently of fitness
differences, but is only weakly identified by two samples; when estimated
freely, it drifts upwards and inflates all fitness differences. It is
therefore specified as $b_0$ in (\ref{eq:coef-prior}). Its effect can be
characterised. The fitness difference between two well-sampled clones is
determined by their frequency change and does not depend on $b_0$. The
fitness deficit of a rare or unobserved karyotype is determined by its
abundance; by (\ref{eq:balance-limit}) with $M_{jp}=2P_{pj}b_p$, it is fixed
per division,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d_p-d_j}{b_p}\approx\frac{2P_{pj}\,n_p}{n_j},
  \label{eq:per-division}
\end{equation}
and is therefore proportional to $b_0$ per unit time. Rankings are largely
unaffected. In simulations, a rate four to ten times too low still yielded
more accurate predictions than the null predictor that assumes no fitness
differences (Section~\ref{sec:validation}), whereas a rate four times too high
yielded less accurate ones. Neither the marginal
likelihood of a single sample nor that of a cohort identified the rate.
Accordingly, \texttt{fit\_sparse\_joint\_rates()} fits the model at several
rates across a plausible range and reports relative fitness at the
\emph{lowest} rate, an interval covering all fitted rates, and fitness per
division, $\rho_i/b_0$, over the range.

\subsection{Joint Analysis of Multiple Samples}
\label{sec:sj-cohort}

\texttt{fit\_sparse\_joint\_cohort()} analyses several samples jointly. Each
sample $s$ retains its own domain $\mathcal{D}_s$, initial abundances, depth
ratio and observation model. A karyotype $v$ present in any domain has a
shared log birth rate,
\begin{equation}
  m_v\sim\Normal\Bigl(\frac{1}{|S_v|}\sum_{s\in S_v}z_{sv}^\top\gamma_s,\ \sigma_r^2\Bigr),
  \qquad
  g_{sv}\sim\Normal\bigl(m_v,\ \tau^2\bigr),\quad v\in\mathcal{D}_s,
  \label{eq:cohort}
\end{equation}
where $S_v$ is the set of samples whose domain contains $v$, and the
between-sample scale $\tau$ is estimated together with the other scales
(\texttt{sharing = "partial"}); alternatively, $g_{sv}=m_v$
(\texttt{sharing = "full"}). A karyotype that is abundant in one sample and an
unobserved neighbour in another is thus informed by both. With one sample and
full sharing, the model reduces to the single-sample model.

The trend is specified either per sample or jointly. In the per-sample
specification, $z_{sv}$ is centred on the mean karyotype of sample $s$, its
distance term is measured to the major karyotypes of that sample, and each
$\gamma_s$ has prior (\ref{eq:coef-prior}). In the joint specification,
$\gamma_s=\gamma$ for all samples, with covariates centred on the average of
the sample means and distances measured to the major karyotypes of all
samples. Both specifications share the same hyperparameters, and by default
the one with the higher Laplace marginal likelihood is selected. In simulated
cohorts, a joint trend improved estimates for samples from a common landscape
but substantially degraded them for samples from different landscapes; the
marginal likelihood selected per-sample trends for 11 of 12 cohorts drawn
from different landscapes. Cohorts use independent initial abundances by
default. Under the balance prior, the outer optimisation failed at the
prior-centred hyperparameters for 2 of 12 simulated development cohorts,
leaving no marginal likelihood by which to select the trend; for the
remaining 10, it reduced the error at distances 1 and 2 relative to
independent initial abundances.

\subsection{Export in the \texttt{alfakR} Output Format}
\label{sec:sj-alfakR}

\texttt{as\_alfakR()} and \texttt{alfak\_compat()} generate the objects saved
by \texttt{alfakR::alfak()} (Section~\ref{sec:alfakR}), with identical names,
columns and karyotype sets.

\paragraph{Karyotype sets and scale.}
The frequent set, its one-step neighbours without nullisomy, and the
landscape are defined as
\begin{equation}
  F=\{v:y_{0v}+y_{1v}\ge\texttt{minobs}\},\qquad
  N=\partial F\setminus F,\qquad
  K=F\cup N\cup\partial N,
  \label{eq:alfakR-sets}
\end{equation}
where $\partial A$ denotes the set of one-step neighbours of $A$ with all copy
numbers at least one, in the order used by \texttt{alfakR}. The value of
\texttt{minobs} affects these sets only, not the fit. Reported fitness is
\begin{equation}
  f_v=\rho_v+\frac{\log(N_b/N_0^{\mathrm{pop}})}{T}
  \quad\text{or, with \texttt{correct\_efflux},}\quad
  f_v=\frac{\rho_v+\log(N_b/N_0^{\mathrm{pop}})/T}{2P_{vv}-1},
  \label{eq:alfakR-scale}
\end{equation}
where $N_0^{\mathrm{pop}}$ and $N_b$ are the population sizes \texttt{n0} and
\texttt{nb} of \texttt{alfakR}; the mean fitness of the major karyotypes is
thereby set to the population growth rate assumed by \texttt{alfakR}.

\paragraph{Posterior draws.}
In place of bootstrap replicates, $B$ draws are generated for every
$v\in K$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Draw $\delta u=P^\top L^{-\top}\epsilon$ with
$\epsilon\sim\Normal(0,I)$, which has covariance $H_E^{-1}$.
\item Map the draw to log birth rates: $\delta g_v$ for $v\in\mathcal{D}$;
$z(x_v)^\top\delta\gamma+\hat\sigma_r\eta_v$ for trend predictions; and
$\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}}\,\eta_v$ from (\ref{eq:predict-level}) for observed
karyotypes outside the domain, with $\eta_v\sim\Normal(0,1)$.
\item Linearise:
$\delta\rho_v=\hat d_v\,\delta g_v-\sum_mw_m\hat d_m\,\delta g_m$, so that the
reference is drawn jointly with each karyotype.
\item Add independent noise with variance $\tilde s_v^2-s_v^2$, the component
of the calibrated variance not represented by the model.
\item Use each draw together with its reflection, $\pm\delta\rho$, add the
posterior mean when $B$ is odd, and rescale each row to standard deviation
$\tilde s_v$.
\end{enumerate}
The mean and median of each row then equal the prediction exactly, and its
standard deviation equals $\tilde s_v$, while the correlations between
karyotypes are those implied by the model. \texttt{landscape.Rds} contains
these row summaries; \texttt{bootstrap\_res.Rds} contains the columns of $F$
and $N$, together with initial frequencies of $F$ derived from the drawn
$\zeta$.

\paragraph{Cross-validation.}
The folds follow \texttt{alfakR}: each frequent karyotype together with those
of its one-step neighbours not assigned to an earlier fold. For a fold $Q$,
the states of $Q$ within the domain are treated as unobserved: $w_i=0$ in
(\ref{eq:poisson}); a $\zeta_i$ with prior (\ref{eq:free-prior}) takes its
third case (under the balance prior, non-major states retain
(\ref{eq:balance-prior}), which does not involve their counts); and $\bar x$
and $\mathcal{M}$ in (\ref{eq:design}) are recomputed without $Q$. The mode
$\hat u_{-Q}$ is recomputed with $\hat\psi$ fixed, and the prediction for
$i\in Q$ is aligned to the full fit through the major karyotypes outside $Q$,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde\rho_i=\hat d_{i,-Q}-\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}\setminus Q}w'_m\bigl(\hat d_{m,-Q}-\hat d_m\bigr)-R,
  \qquad w'_m\propto w_m .
  \label{eq:xval-align}
\end{equation}
With $o_i=f_i$ estimated from all data and $p_i$ the prediction on the same
scale, both centred,
\begin{equation}
  R^2_{\mathrm{xv}}=1-\frac{\sum_i(p_i-o_i)^2}{\sum_io_i^2}.
  \label{eq:r2r}
\end{equation}
Folds containing all major karyotypes are omitted, and karyotypes outside the
domain, whose estimates do not derive from their own counts, are not
evaluated. $R^2_{\mathrm{xv}}$ quantifies how accurately the model predicts
karyotypes whose own counts and those of their neighbours were withheld; it
does not quantify the accuracy of the full fit. It is not comparable with the
corresponding statistic of \texttt{alfakR}, which quantifies how accurately
Kriging interpolates first-stage estimates.

\section{Relation to \texttt{alfakR}}
\label{sec:alfakR}

\subsection{The \texttt{alfakR} Model}

\texttt{alfakR} (ALFA-K; branch \texttt{dev}, commit \texttt{eddeb5e})
estimates the landscape in four stages.

\paragraph{Frequent karyotypes.}
Karyotypes with at least \texttt{minobs} cells across all samples, $F$, evolve
under selection without mutation among themselves,
\begin{equation}
  x_i(t)=\frac{x_{0i}e^{f_it}}{\sum_{k\in F}x_{0k}e^{f_kt}},\qquad
  -\log L=-\sum_t\sum_{i\in F}y_{it}\log x_i(t),\qquad \sum_{i\in F}f_i=0,
  \label{eq:alfakR-freq}
\end{equation}
that is, a multinomial likelihood for the counts of $F$ renormalised within
$F$; cells of other karyotypes do not contribute. Initial values are obtained
from a quadratic programme on frequency derivatives. Fitness is subsequently
shifted such that $\sum_ix_{0i}f_i=\log(N_b/N_0^{\mathrm{pop}})/\Delta t$.

\paragraph{Neighbouring karyotypes.}
Each $p\in F$ is assigned an emergence time $t_p$, at which the fitted
trajectory (\ref{eq:alfakR-freq}), extrapolated backwards, attains frequency
$1/N_0^{\mathrm{pop}}$. Each one-step neighbour $c\in N$ is then estimated
separately, conditional on the point estimates of its parents:
\begin{equation}
  x_c(t)=\sum_{p\in F,\,p\sim c}\hat x_p(t)\,P_{pc}\,f_p\,
  \frac{e^{(f_c-f_p)(t-t_p)}-1}{f_c-f_p},
  \qquad
  y_{ct}\sim\operatorname{Binomial}\bigl(N_t,\,x_c(t)\bigr),
  \label{eq:alfakR-nn}
\end{equation}
by one-dimensional optimisation over $f_c$. An unobserved neighbour
additionally receives an empirical prior on $f_c-\bar f_{\mathrm{parents}}$
estimated from the observed neighbours.

\paragraph{Uncertainty and landscape.}
Both stages are repeated on multinomial resamples of the counts. Kriging
(\texttt{fields::Krig}) on copy-number coordinates, fitted to the resampled
fitness values of $F\cup N$, predicts the landscape on $K$ as defined in
(\ref{eq:alfakR-sets}); the predictions across replicates constitute
\texttt{landscape\_posterior\_samples.Rds} and their summaries
\texttt{landscape.Rds}. Cross-validation withholds each frequent karyotype and
its neighbours from the Kriging and predicts them from the remainder.

\subsection{Methodological Differences}

Both methods employ count likelihoods; the principal difference lies not in
the use of counts versus frequencies but in how the karyotypes are estimated.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{L{0.2\textwidth}L{0.35\textwidth}L{0.35\textwidth}}
\toprule
 & \texttt{alfakR} & \texttt{alfak2} sparse joint model \\
\midrule
Estimation & Sequential: frequent karyotypes, then each neighbour
conditional on fixed parent estimates, then Kriging & A single likelihood for
all states and the trend \\
Information flow & Unidirectional, from parents to neighbours; the counts of a
neighbour do not inform its parent & Bidirectional, through the flux between
all neighbouring states \\
Dynamics among frequent karyotypes & Selection only & Selection and
missegregation \\
Data included & Frequent karyotypes ($\ge$\texttt{minobs} cells) and their
neighbours; other cells excluded & Major karyotypes ($\ge1\%$ and $\ge3$
cells) and all their neighbours, observed or not \\
Abundance of a neighbour & Accumulated since the extrapolated emergence time
of its parent, a fixed function of the fitted trajectory & Balance prior over
a history $h$ with dispersion $\sigma_\zeta$ \\
Prediction beyond the data & Kriging of first-stage estimates & Trend
estimated within the same likelihood \\
Uncertainty & Bootstrap of counts and Kriging & Laplace approximation with
calibration \\
Absolute level & Fixed by $\log(N_b/N_0^{\mathrm{pop}})/\Delta t$ & Relative
fitness; specified birth rate \\
Time points & Any number & Two \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The neighbour model (\ref{eq:alfakR-nn}) of \texttt{alfakR} already relates
the abundance of a rare karyotype to that of its parent; the sparse joint
model retains this principle and embeds it, together with all other states,
in a single likelihood. On the simulated benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:validation}),
\texttt{alfakR} with \texttt{minobs = 20} exceeded the error of the null
predictor that assumes no fitness differences at every wavelength and
distance, largely because of a small number of poorly constrained fits,
whereas the sparse joint model reduced it to 0.47--0.74 of that value.

separately in Rcpp.

\subsection{Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:val-design}

\paragraph{Simulation.}
Fitness landscapes are the Gaussian random fields of the ALFA-K simulations,
\begin{equation}
  f(x)=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sin\Bigl(\frac{\lVert x-c_k\rVert_2}{\lambda}\Bigr),
  \qquad K=10,
  \label{eq:grf}
\end{equation}
where the centroids $c_k$ have integer coordinates drawn uniformly from
$\{-10,\ldots,20\}$, $\lambda\in\{0.4,0.8,1.2,1.6\}$ is the wavelength, and
$f(x)$ is the division rate per day; centroids are redrawn until the diploid
founder divides faster than 0.4 per day. Populations with 22 chromosomes
evolve from the diploid founder in an agent-based model with time step
$\Delta t=0.1$ days: in each step a cell divides with probability
$f(x)\Delta t$, each chromosome copy missegregates with probability $\beta$,
daughters with a copy number outside $[1,8]$ are inviable, and populations
reaching $2\times10^6$ cells are reduced to 1\% at random. Two samples are
drawn from each population under eight scenarios, with 50 to 3000 cells per
sample and intervals of 180 to 1080 days, including unbalanced sample sizes
and a bottleneck. The first sample is drawn on day 1440. Each sampled cell
contributes one karyotype, without calling error, and the input to every
method is the resulting two-column count matrix. The number of distinct
karyotypes observed in the two samples together increases with sample size
and with landscape smoothness, because populations on smooth landscapes are
more diverse. Each row of the following table is one sampling design
(scenario); values are medians and ranges over the six landscapes of each
wavelength, development and held-out:

\noindent Across all datasets, 59\% of observed karyotypes (median) were seen

divided into twelve development landscapes, used for model construction, and
twelve held-out landscapes, which were evaluated once after the method had
been fixed. On each landscape one population trajectory is simulated and
sampled under each of the eight scenarios (the bottleneck scenario regrows
from the first sample), giving 96 datasets per set; results are averaged
within landscape and scenario and then over landscapes with equal weight.

\paragraph{Reference values and scoring.}
Because the landscape is known, the true fitness of every karyotype, observed
or not, can be computed. The reference value is the growth rate that the
simulated counts follow,
\begin{equation}
  r(x)=\frac{\log\bigl(1+\max\{f(x),0\}\,\Delta t\bigr)}{\Delta t},
  \label{eq:true-rate}
\end{equation}
which removes the bias of the discrete time step. Each dataset is scored on
all observed karyotypes and on random samples of up to 2000 one-step and 2000
two-step neighbours, selected from the counts alone and common to all
methods. Reference values and predictions $\hat f$ are centred on the same
set of reference karyotypes, the observed karyotypes with at least five cells
over both samples, weighted by their counts:
\begin{equation}
  \rho^{\mathrm{true}}_x=r(x)-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}}w_vr(v),\qquad
  \rho^{\mathrm{pred}}_x=\hat f(x)-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}}w_v\hat f(v),\qquad
  w_v=\frac{y_{0v}+y_{1v}}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{R}}(y_{0u}+y_{1u})}.
  \label{eq:scoring-centre}
\end{equation}
The reference set depends only on the counts. Centring removes the absolute
level, which two samples do not identify, and places all methods on a common
origin irrespective of their internal reference. A method that provides no
prediction for a reference karyotype is scored as the null predictor on that
dataset.

\paragraph{Aggregation.}
For landscape $L$, scenario $S$ and distance $d$, the error is computed over
the scored karyotypes at that distance, each weighted equally,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RMSE}_{L,S,d}=\Bigl(\frac{1}{n_d}\sum_{x:\,\mathrm{dist}(x)=d}
  \bigl(\rho^{\mathrm{pred}}_x-\rho^{\mathrm{true}}_x\bigr)^2\Bigr)^{1/2},
  \label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
where $n_d$ is the number of observed karyotypes (median 103 per dataset) for
$d=0$ and 2000 for $d=1,2$. Because fitness differences on rugged landscapes
($\lambda=0.4$) are about ten times larger than on smooth ones
($\lambda=1.6$), results are reported separately for each wavelength. For
wavelength $\lambda$, with $\Lambda_\lambda$ its three landscapes, errors are
averaged over the eight scenarios of each landscape and then over the
landscapes,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RMSE}_{\lambda,d}=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{L\in\Lambda_\lambda}\frac{1}{8}\sum_S\mathrm{RMSE}_{L,S,d},
  \label{eq:rmse-pooled}
\end{equation}
and rank correlations and coverage likewise. Each value is thus the
equally weighted mean of 24 datasets, three landscapes by eight sampling
designs, so that every design counts equally irrespective of its sample size
or number of karyotypes. Landscapes are the independent units: the scenarios of a landscape are drawn from one simulated trajectory.
Comparisons between methods are therefore paired by landscape, and confidence
intervals are obtained by resampling landscapes. For patient samples no
reference values exist, and the assessments described below are indirect.

\paragraph{Reference predictor.}
The null predictor, denoted \emph{no fitness differences}, assigns a relative
fitness of zero to every karyotype and uses no data. Because reference values
are centred, its root mean squared error equals the root mean square of the
true relative fitness, that is, the typical magnitude of the fitness
differences to be estimated at each distance. A method that does not attain a
lower error than this predictor extracts no usable information on fitness
differences. It serves as the benchmark for error in preference to
\texttt{alfakR}, whose error is dominated by a small number of poorly
constrained fits.

\label{sec:val-wavelength}

The following tables report, for each wavelength $\lambda$ and distance $d$
from the observed karyotypes, the root mean squared error of relative
fitness, the Spearman correlation with the reference values and the coverage
of nominal 95\% intervals, each computed as in (\ref{eq:rmse-pooled}).
Coverage is the fraction of scored karyotypes whose reference value lies
within the nominal 95\% interval of a method, the prediction plus or minus
1.96 standard deviations; a calibrated method attains 0.95, and lower values
indicate overconfident, higher values overly wide intervals. The null
predictor provides neither a ranking nor intervals. The intervals of

over bootstrap replicates; karyotypes outside its landscape have none and are

\subsection{Additional Assessments}
\label{sec:val-additional}

Predicted compositions 180 days after the second sample were closer to the
simulated compositions than the assumption of no change (total variation
distance 0.251 versus 0.290). Increasing the sample size to 20\,000 cells per
sample reduced the error by only 14--21\%, to the level of an oracle that
predicts the true mean of each distance stratum; accuracy is therefore limited
by the information contained in two time points rather than by sample size.
All 20 GBM samples were fitted at three birth rates, and random halves of
each sample yielded rankings with a Spearman correlation of 0.88 for
karyotypes with at least ten cells. Detailed results are reported in
\texttt{validation/sparse\_joint/README.md}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:val-limitations}

All simulated landscapes, including the held-out ones, belong to a single
family, the Gaussian random fields (\ref{eq:grf}); the calibration constants
of (\ref{eq:calibration}) were estimated on the same family. The results
therefore establish generalisation to new landscapes of this family, not to
landscapes of other structure, such as predominantly additive landscapes or
landscapes with strong local epistasis. The simulations include neither
karyotype-calling errors nor spatial sampling bias, and treatment is
represented only by a bottleneck and by changes of the landscape at the first
sample. The summary statistics of the simulated samples overlap with those of
20 glioblastoma samples, although the simulated samples contain more
karyotypes observed in a single cell. The benchmark is thus a necessary test
of the method, not evidence of its accuracy on patient samples, for which no
reference values exist.

\section{Summary}

The default pipeline of \texttt{alfak2} can be summarised as
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\text{counts at two time points}
+ \text{closed-form division dynamics}
+ \text{mutation--selection balance}\\
&\longrightarrow
\text{relative fitness of the major karyotypes and their neighbours}\\
&\longrightarrow
\text{trend-based prediction beyond them},
\end{aligned}
\]
with calibrated uncertainty and, optionally, output in the \texttt{alfakR}
format. The earlier pipelines are described in the Supplement.

\section{The Two-Layer Pipeline}
\label{sec:s-two-layer}

The two-layer pipeline, \texttt{fit\_alfak2(..., pipeline = "two\_layer")},
was the default before November 2026 and remains available. It first fits a
local hierarchical count model around the observed karyotypes and then treats
the resulting local fitness estimates as noisy anchors, from which a
second-layer method predicts fitness on a larger bounded graph:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{counts}&\longrightarrow\text{local count model}\longrightarrow
\text{anchors }(z_a,s_a^2)\\
&\longrightarrow\text{second-layer method}\longrightarrow\text{global landscape}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The second layer does not use the counts. On the sparse two-sample benchmark,
the pipeline with its default second layer was not more accurate than the
null predictor that assumes no fitness differences
(Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

\subsection{Procedure}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Input depth.} The counts are used as supplied or resampled to an
effective depth (Section~\ref{sec:s-preprocessing}).
\item \textbf{Local graph.} The observed karyotypes and their neighbours up to
\texttt{local\_shell\_depth} steps (default 2), with copy numbers in $[0,5]$
and at most 5000 nodes (Section~\ref{sec:s-graph}).
\item \textbf{Transport kernel} on this graph, exact by default
(Section~\ref{sec:kernel}).
\item \textbf{Local posterior.} The local hierarchical count model, compiled as
the TMB model \texttt{local\_model}, with a multinomial observation model for
raw counts and a Dirichlet-multinomial observation model for effective counts
(Section~\ref{sec:s-local}).
\item \textbf{Anchors.} Local estimates with finite variances, inflated
according to the status of their covariance and, optionally, for low counts
(Section~\ref{sec:s-anchors}).
\item \textbf{Global graph.} The local graph extended by
\texttt{global\_extra\_shell} steps (default 1), or an explicitly specified
set of target karyotypes.
\item \textbf{Second layer.} One of the nine methods of
Supplement~\ref{sec:s-extrapolation}, by default
\texttt{graph\_gaussian\_baseline}, with hyperparameters selected by
leave-one-anchor-out cross-validation when grids are supplied.
\item \textbf{Output}, optionally on the \texttt{alfakR} scale
(Sections~\ref{sec:s-output}--\ref{sec:s-alfakR-scale}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Count Preprocessing}
\label{sec:s-preprocessing}

If \texttt{input\_depth = "raw"}, the model uses the supplied count matrix
directly:
\[
  y'_{vt}=y_{vt}.
\]

If \texttt{input\_depth = "effective"}, each timepoint is first converted to
frequencies and then recounted at a controlled target depth:
\[
  N_t = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{O}} y_{vt},
  \qquad
  \hat p_{vt} = \frac{y_{vt}}{N_t}.
\]
The target depth is
\[
  D_t =
  \begin{cases}
  \min(N_0,N_1), & \text{mode = min},\\
  \min(N_t,D), & \text{mode = cap},\\
  D, & \text{mode = fixed}.
  \end{cases}
\]
The effective counts satisfy
\[
  y'_{vt} \approx D_t \hat p_{vt},
  \qquad
  \sum_v y'_{vt}=D_t.
\]
Rounding is performed with a largest-remainder style rule so that the total is
exactly preserved.

This step controls the influence of sequencing depth. Without it, a timepoint
with much larger depth can dominate the likelihood even when the biological
information content should be treated as comparable.

\subsection{Bounded Karyotype Graph}
\label{sec:s-graph}

\texttt{alfak2} builds a finite graph
$\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$ around the observed karyotypes.
Two nodes are connected by a one-step edge if they differ by one copy on one
chromosome:
\[
  x_v = x_u \pm e_c,
\]
where $e_c$ is the unit vector in chromosome coordinate $c$.

Graph expansion is bounded by copy-number limits, shell depth, and a maximum
node count:
\[
  \min_{\mathrm{cn}} \le x_{vc} \le \max_{\mathrm{cn}},
  \qquad
  d(v) \le h,
  \qquad
  |\mathcal{V}| \le M.
\]

Nodes are classified by support distance:
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
d(v)=0 & \text{directly informed},\\
d(v)=1 & \text{local borrowed},\\
d(v)=2 & \text{weakly supported},\\
d(v)>2 & \text{graph borrowed}.
\end{array}
\]

The bounded graph avoids enumerating the full copy-number lattice, whose size
grows exponentially with chromosome dimension. It keeps the inference focused
on biologically plausible states near the observed karyotypes.

\subsection{Missegregation Transition Kernel}
\label{sec:kernel}

\subsubsection{Exact Single-Chromosome Kernel}

Let $i$ be the parent copy number and $j$ the child copy number for one
chromosome. Under the exact kernel, the transition probability is
\[
  p_{\beta}(i \to j)
  =
  \sum_{\substack{m=|i-j|\\m\equiv |i-j|\;(\mathrm{mod}\;2)}}^{i}
  \binom{i}{m}
  \beta^m(1-\beta)^{i-m}
  2^{-m}
  \binom{m}{(m+i-j)/2}.
\]
Here $m$ is the number of missegregating copies. The term
$\binom{i}{m}$ selects which copies missegregate,
$\beta^m(1-\beta)^{i-m}$ is the missegregation probability, and
$2^{-m}\binom{m}{(m+i-j)/2}$ accounts for their allocation to the two
daughter directions.

\subsubsection{Karyotype-Level Transition Weight}

For a full karyotype transition from node $u$ to node $v$, the exact weight is
the product over chromosomes:
\[
  q_{\beta}(u \to v)
  =
  \prod_{c=1}^{p}
  p_{\beta}(x_{uc}\to x_{vc}).
\]

The alternative \texttt{linear} kernel retains the first-order
approximation used in earlier versions:
\[
  q_{\beta}(u \to v)
  =
  \begin{cases}
  \beta\max(1,x_{uc}), & x_v=x_u\pm e_c,\\
  1, & v=u,\\
  0, & \text{otherwise}.
  \end{cases}
\]

\subsubsection{Row-Stochastic Transport Matrix}

The local forward model uses a row-stochastic transport matrix $T$. Let
$q_{uv}$ be the one-step edge weight and $s_u$ the self weight. Then
\[
  T_{uv}
  =
  \frac{q_{uv}}{s_u+\sum_{z:(u,z)\in\mathcal{E}}q_{uz}},
  \qquad u\ne v,
\]
and
\[
  T_{uu}
  =
  \frac{s_u}{s_u+\sum_{z:(u,z)\in\mathcal{E}}q_{uz}}.
\]
For the exact kernel, $s_u=q_\beta(u\to u)$. For the linear kernel, $s_u=1$.

\subsection{Local Hierarchical Bayesian Count Model}
\label{sec:s-local}

The local model estimates initial frequencies and fitness values on a local
karyotype graph $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{local}}$.

\subsubsection{Initial Frequency Parameterization}

Each node $v$ has an initial log-abundance parameter $\eta_v$. Initial
frequencies are obtained by a softmax transform:
\[
  \pi_{0v}
  =
  \frac{\exp(\eta_v)}
       {\sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}}\exp(\eta_u)}.
\]

\subsubsection{Selection and Missegregation Forward Model}

Fitness acts through exponential growth between the two timepoints:
\[
  g_v = \pi_{0v}\exp(\Delta t\,f_v).
\]
After growth, mass is transported through the missegregation matrix:
\[
  \tilde\pi_{1v}
  =
  \sum_{u\in\mathcal{V}} T_{uv}g_u.
\]
The result is normalized to a frequency vector:
\[
  \pi_{1v}
  =
  \frac{\tilde\pi_{1v}}
       {\sum_{z\in\mathcal{V}}\tilde\pi_{1z}}.
\]

Thus the model explicitly represents
\[
  \text{initial composition}
  \quad+\quad
  \text{fitness-driven growth}
  \quad+\quad
  \text{missegregation transport}.
\]

\subsubsection{Observation Model}

For raw counts, the default observation model is multinomial:
\[
  y_{\cdot 0}\mid \pi_0
  \sim
  \Mult(N_0,\pi_0),
  \qquad
  y_{\cdot 1}\mid \pi_1
  \sim
  \Mult(N_1,\pi_1),
\]
where $N_t=\sum_v y_{vt}$. Up to constants independent of the parameters,
\[
  \ell_{\mathrm{mult}}
  =
  \sum_v y_{v0}\log \pi_{0v}
  +
  \sum_v y_{v1}\log \pi_{1v}.
\]

The optional Dirichlet-multinomial observation model is
\[
  y_{\cdot t}\mid \pi_t,\phi
  \sim
  \DM(N_t,\pi_t,\phi),
\]
with log-likelihood
\[
  \ell_{\mathrm{DM}}(y,\pi,\phi)
  =
  \log\Gamma(\phi)
  -
  \log\Gamma(N+\phi)
  +
  \sum_v
  \left[
    \log\Gamma(y_v+\phi\pi_v)
    -
    \log\Gamma(\phi\pi_v)
  \right].
\]
This model allows overdispersion relative to the multinomial likelihood.

\subsubsection{Prior for Initial Abundance}

Initial log-abundance has a node-specific Gaussian prior:
\[
  \eta_v \sim \Normal(m_{\eta v},s_{\eta v}^2).
\]
Directly observed or otherwise effectively counted nodes use a diffuse prior:
\[
  m_{\eta v}=0,\qquad s_{\eta v}=5.
\]
Unobserved borrowed nodes use a smaller prior mean:
\[
  m_{\eta v}
  =
  -6 - 0.75\max\{0,d(v)-1\},
  \qquad
  s_{\eta v}=1.5.
\]
This encodes the conservative assumption that unobserved nearby karyotypes may
exist but should have small initial abundance unless the data support them.

\subsubsection{Hierarchical Fitness Prior}

For directly informed nodes, fitness is centered at zero:
\[
  f_v\mid \sigma_{\mathrm{anchor}}
  \sim
  \Normal(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{anchor}}^2),
  \qquad d(v)=0.
\]

Borrowed nodes receive information from parent nodes closer to the observed
set. Each parent edge $(u,v)$ has a context
\[
  c(u,v)
  =
  \{\text{gain/loss},\;\text{chromosome index},\;\text{parent ploidy band}\}.
\]
Context effects follow a hierarchical Gaussian prior:
\[
  \delta_c\mid \mu_{g(c)},\tau_{g(c)}
  \sim
  \Normal(\mu_{g(c)},\tau_{g(c)}^2),
\]
\[
  \mu_g\sim \Normal(0,0.5^2).
\]
The scale parameters are positive and are given half-normal type priors:
\[
  \sigma_{\mathrm{neighbor}}>0,\qquad
  \sigma_{\mathrm{anchor}}>0,\qquad
  \tau_g>0.
\]

For a borrowed node $v$, the prior mean is the transition-weighted average of
parent fitness plus context effect:
\[
  m_{fv}
  =
  \frac{
    \sum_{u\in P(v)}
    w_{uv}\{f_u+\delta_{c(u,v)}\}
  }{
    \sum_{u\in P(v)}w_{uv}
  }.
\]
The borrowed-node prior is
\[
  f_v\mid \{f_u:u\in P(v)\},\delta,\sigma_{\mathrm{neighbor}}
  \sim
  \Normal(m_{fv},\sigma_{\mathrm{neighbor}}^2),
  \qquad d(v)>0.
\]
When $d(v)\ge 2$, the local model inflates this prior standard deviation by
$1.75$:
\[
  f_v \sim \Normal(m_{fv},(1.75\sigma_{\mathrm{neighbor}})^2).
\]
This expresses weaker local information farther from observed nodes.

\subsubsection{Local Posterior Objective}

The local layer estimates the maximum a posteriori parameter vector
\[
  \theta=(\eta,f,\delta,\mu,\sigma_{\mathrm{neighbor}},
  \sigma_{\mathrm{anchor}},\tau).
\]
It solves
\[
  \hat\theta
  =
  \arg\max_{\theta}
  \left\{
    \ell(Y\mid \eta,f,\beta,\Delta t)
    +
    \log p(\eta)
    +
    \log p(f\mid \delta)
    +
    \log p(\delta,\mu,\sigma,\tau)
  \right\}.
\]
Equivalently, the code minimizes the negative log posterior
\[
  \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{local}}(\theta)
  =
  -\ell(Y\mid \eta,f,\beta,\Delta t)
  -
  \log p(\eta)
  -
  \log p(f\mid \delta)
  -
  \log p(\delta,\mu,\sigma,\tau).
\]

This objective is implemented with Template Model Builder (TMB). TMB is not the
statistical algorithm; it is the numerical backend used to compute derivatives,
optimize the MAP objective, and obtain approximate marginal standard errors
through \texttt{sdreport}.

The local layer returns, for each local graph node,
\[
  z_v=\hat f_v,\qquad s_v=\widehat{\mathrm{sd}}(f_v).
\]
These become noisy anchors for the global graph layer.

\subsection{Output of the Two-Layer Pipeline}
\label{sec:s-output}

The local layer reports
\[
  \hat f_v^{\mathrm{local}},
  \qquad
  \widehat{\operatorname{sd}}(f_v^{\mathrm{local}}),
  \qquad
  \hat\pi_{0v},
  \qquad
  \hat\pi_{1v}.
\]
The second layer reports
\[
  \hat f_v^{\mathrm{global}},
  \qquad
  \widehat{\operatorname{sd}}(f_v^{\mathrm{global}}),
  \qquad
  \texttt{prediction\_status}_v.
\]
For \texttt{graph\_gaussian\_baseline} and
\texttt{truncated\_nearfield\_gmrf}, the estimate is the Gaussian posterior
mean $\mu_v$ and, when \texttt{compute\_sd = TRUE},
\[
  \widehat{\operatorname{sd}}(f_v^{\mathrm{global}})
  =
  \sqrt{(\bm Q^{-1})_{vv}}.
\]
For the other near-field methods, the reported standard deviation is
method-specific: path ensembles combine anchor, edge-effect, and between-path
variance; local GP uses conditional predictive variance; local polynomial uses
weighted residual and leverage variance; delta-feature models use residual
delta-model variance. These are uncertainty estimates on the common output
schema, not all exact Gaussian posterior marginals.
Approximate normal intervals are
\[
  \hat f_v \pm 1.9599639845\,\widehat{\operatorname{sd}}(f_v).
\]

The fit also reports support tier, support distance, anchor diagnostics,
selected hyperparameters, tuning grid, dependency status, convergence status,
runtime, and method-specific diagnostics. Nodes far from anchors can be labeled
\texttt{out\_of\_scope} by near-field methods. In the full graph Gaussian
posterior, nodes far from direct support can be labeled graph-borrowed or
prior-dominated, indicating that their estimates are driven mostly by prior
structure rather than direct data.

\subsection{Optional \texttt{alfakR}-Scale Transformation}
\label{sec:s-alfakR-scale}

This option belongs to the two-layer pipeline; the default pipeline produces
\texttt{alfakR}'s outputs as described in Section~\ref{sec:sj-alfakR}.
Native \texttt{alfak2} fitness is on a relative scale. If
\texttt{alfakR\_scale = TRUE}, the package appends legacy-scale columns for
compatibility with \texttt{alfakR}. Let
\[
  g_0=\frac{\log(n_b/n_0)}{\Delta t}
\]
and define the efflux viability approximation
\[
  V(x)=2(1-\beta)^{\sum_c x_c}-1.
\]

Without efflux correction,
\[
  f^{\mathrm{alfakR}}(x)
  =
  f^{\mathrm{alfak2}}(x)
  +
  g_0
  -
  \sum_x w_x f^{\mathrm{alfak2}}(x).
\]
With efflux correction,
\[
  o
  =
  \frac{
    \sum_x w_x f^{\mathrm{alfak2}}(x)/V(x)-g_0
  }{
    \sum_x w_x/V(x)
  },
\]
\[
  f^{\mathrm{alfakR}}(x)
  =
  \frac{f^{\mathrm{alfak2}}(x)-o}{V(x)}.
\]
This is a post-processing scale conversion. It does not change the native
\texttt{alfak2} posterior estimates.

\subsection{Design Considerations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Count likelihoods.} The local layer models counts with
  multinomial or Dirichlet-multinomial likelihoods.
  \item \textbf{Separation of selection and missegregation.} The forward model
  scales $\pi_0$ by $\exp(\Delta t\,f)$ and then applies a single
  missegregation step $T^\top$.
  \item \textbf{Bounded state space.} Bounded graph expansion avoids
  enumeration of the full copy-number lattice.
  \item \textbf{Alternative second layers.} Graph Gaussian posteriors,
  near-field regression and tabular models are available.
  \item \textbf{Propagation of uncertainty.} Uncertainty from the local TMB
  layer is propagated to the second layer, and support tiers distinguish
  directly informed from graph-borrowed estimates.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Second-Layer Extrapolation Methods}
\label{sec:s-extrapolation}

The second layer of the two-layer pipeline is selected with
\texttt{extrapolation\_method}. All nine methods take the same anchors, namely
the local fitness estimates and their variances, and predict fitness on the
same bounded graph; none of them uses the counts. They are not part of the
default pipeline.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{L{0.42\textwidth}L{0.5\textwidth}}
\toprule
Method & Principle \\
\midrule
\texttt{graph\_gaussian\_baseline} & Gaussian posterior on the full graph with
edge smoothing, copy-number curvature and pairwise epistasis penalties \\
\texttt{truncated\_nearfield\_gmrf} & The same posterior restricted to the
subgraph within distance 2 \\
\texttt{edge\_effect\_empirical\_bayes} & Mean effect of the gain or loss of
each chromosome, with empirical Bayes shrinkage \\
\texttt{edge\_effect\_interaction\_path\_ensemble} & As above, with
corrections for two-step paths \\
\texttt{local\_polynomial\_stencil} & Weighted local quadratic regression on
neighbouring anchors \\
\texttt{local\_NNGP\_or\_GPnn} & Nearest-neighbour Gaussian process with an
exponential kernel in $L_1$ distance \\
\texttt{delta\_tree\_ensemble} & Tree ensemble for parent-to-target fitness
differences based on tabular features \\
\texttt{tabpfn\_nearfield\_feature\_model} & Reserved for TabPFN; in the

\texttt{kronecker\_or\_graph\_trend\_filtering} & Graph trend filtering on

\subsection{Second-Layer Extrapolation Framework}

The second layer does not revisit the raw counts. Instead, it treats local
fitness estimates as noisy anchors and predicts fitness on a larger bounded
graph $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{global}}$. The public entry point is
\texttt{fit\_extrapolation\_layer()}, and \texttt{fit\_alfak2()} passes
\texttt{extrapolation\_method} to this function.

All second-layer methods return the same global-fit schema:
\[
  \{\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{global}},\;
    \text{node summary},\;
    \text{anchor table},\;
    \text{hyperparameters},\;
    \text{tuning grid},\;
    \text{diagnostics}\}.
\]
The node summary always contains
\[
\begin{array}{llll}
\texttt{fitness\_mean}, & \texttt{fitness\_sd}, &
\texttt{conf\_low}, & \texttt{conf\_high},\\
\texttt{support\_distance}, & \texttt{support\_tier}, &
\texttt{extrapolation\_method}, & \texttt{prediction\_status}.
\end{array}
\]

The current method registry is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{graph\_gaussian\_baseline}: full-graph sparse Gaussian posterior.
  \item \texttt{edge\_effect\_empirical\_bayes}: ridge-shrunk one-step edge effect extrapolation.
  \item \texttt{edge\_effect\_interaction\_path\_ensemble}: one- and two-step path ensemble with edge interactions.
  \item \texttt{kronecker\_or\_graph\_trend\_filtering}: near-field graph trend filtering.
  \item \texttt{local\_NNGP\_or\_GPnn}: nearest-neighbor local Gaussian process.
  \item \texttt{delta\_tree\_ensemble}: tabular parent-to-target fitness-delta model.
  \item \texttt{tabpfn\_nearfield\_feature\_model}: TabPFN adapter name with current tree fallback.
  \item \texttt{truncated\_nearfield\_gmrf}: near-field induced-subgraph Gaussian posterior.
  \item \texttt{local\_polynomial\_stencil}: local weighted polynomial stencil regression.
\end{itemize}
The accepted legacy aliases are:
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
\texttt{graph\_gaussian} &\mapsto& \texttt{graph\_gaussian\_baseline},\\
\texttt{nearfield\_local\_edge\_effect} &\mapsto& \texttt{edge\_effect\_empirical\_bayes},\\
\texttt{edge\_interaction\_path\_ensemble} &\mapsto& \texttt{edge\_effect\_interaction\_path\_ensemble},\\
\texttt{graph\_trend\_filtering} &\mapsto& \texttt{kronecker\_or\_graph\_trend\_filtering}.
\end{array}
\]

The default reliable near-field reporting distance is
\[
  d(v)\le d_{\max}=2.
\]
For every method except \texttt{graph\_gaussian\_baseline}, nodes with
$d(v)>d_{\max}$ are marked \texttt{out\_of\_scope} and their reported fitness
is set to missing. This is deliberate: these methods are intended to estimate
directly informed and one- or two-hop neighboring karyotypes, not arbitrary
far-field graph states.

\subsubsection{Shared Anchor Construction and Observation Model}
\label{sec:s-anchors}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be the retained anchor set. For each anchor $a$,
\[
  z_a\mid f_a
  \sim
  \Normal(f_a,R_a),
\]
where
\[
  R_a = s_a^2\,m_a + \sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^2.
\]
The multiplier $m_a$ combines covariance-status and count-based inflation:
\[
  m_a
  =
  m^{\mathrm{cov}}_a
  m^{\mathrm{count}}_a.
\]
If count-based downweighting is enabled,
\[
  m^{\mathrm{count}}_a
  =
  \max\left\{
    1,
    \left(\frac{C_{\mathrm{ref}}}{C_a}\right)^\alpha
  \right\}.
\]
Low-count or unreliable local anchors therefore receive larger observation
variance and have less influence on the global posterior.

The retained anchors are controlled by:
\[
  \texttt{anchor\_support\_tiers},\quad
  \texttt{anchor\_exclude},\quad
  \texttt{anchor\_min\_effective\_count}.
\]
\texttt{anchor\_support\_tiers = "all"} keeps all finite local posterior rows.
Otherwise, only the requested support tiers are allowed. \texttt{anchor\_exclude}
removes named karyotypes from the anchor set and is used for holdout
validation. If \texttt{anchor\_min\_effective\_count} is not \texttt{NULL},
only rows with effective count strictly larger than that threshold are retained.

Edge weights entering second-layer methods are selected by
\texttt{graph\_edge\_weight}:
\[
  w_{ij}^{\mathrm{mutation}} = q_{ij},\qquad
  w_{ij}^{\mathrm{unit}} = \mathbf{1}\{q_{ij}>0\},\qquad
  w_{ij}^{\mathrm{normalized}} = q_{ij}/\operatorname{median}(q_{ij}:q_{ij}>0).
\]
The normalized mode keeps relative mutation plausibility but removes the global
scale set by $\beta$.

\subsubsection{\texttt{graph\_gaussian\_baseline}: Full Graph Gaussian Posterior}

This method was the default second layer of the two-layer pipeline. It infers the full fitness vector
\[
  \bm f=(f_1,\ldots,f_n)^\top
\]
on $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{global}}$ with a sparse Gaussian posterior.

\paragraph{Graph Laplacian Smoothing}

The first global prior term is graph edge smoothing:
\[
  \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}(\bm f)
  =
  \sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}}
  w_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2.
\]
It enters the posterior with strength $\lambda_l$:
\[
  \lambda_l\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}(\bm f).
\]
This term encourages neighboring karyotypes connected by plausible one-step
copy-number changes to have similar fitness unless anchor data support a sharp
difference.

\paragraph{Ordered Copy-Number Curvature Penalty}

The second prior term penalizes second differences along a chromosome
copy-number axis. For any triplet $x-e_c$, $x$, $x+e_c$ present in the graph,
define
\[
  \Delta_c^2 f(x)
  =
  f(x-e_c)-2f(x)+f(x+e_c).
\]
The curvature penalty is
\[
  \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{curv}}(\bm f)
  =
  \sum_{x,c}
  \left\{\Delta_c^2 f(x)\right\}^2.
\]
This discourages unsupported zig-zag behavior along ordered copy-number
coordinates.

\paragraph{Epistasis Penalty}

The third prior term controls second-order interaction between two chromosome
directions. For any quadruplet $x$, $x+e_c$, $x+e_d$, and $x+e_c+e_d$ present
in the graph,
\[
  \Delta_{cd} f(x)
  =
  f(x)-f(x+e_c)-f(x+e_d)+f(x+e_c+e_d).
\]
The epistasis penalty is
\[
  \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{epi}}(\bm f)
  =
  \frac{1}{2}
  \sum_{x,c<d}
  \left\{\Delta_{cd}f(x)\right\}^2.
\]
This does not forbid epistasis. It penalizes strong interaction effects when
they are weakly supported by anchors, reducing unstable high-order oscillations
in sparse regions.

\paragraph{Gaussian Posterior}

The global negative log posterior is
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{global}}(\bm f)
  =
  \frac{1}{2}
  \bigg[
  &\lambda_l\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{edge}}(\bm f)
  +
  \lambda_e\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{curv}}(\bm f)
  +
  \lambda_e\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{epi}}(\bm f)\\
  &+
  \sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
  \frac{(f_a-z_a)^2}{R_a}
  +
  \epsilon\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}f_v^2
  \bigg].
\end{aligned}
\]
All terms are quadratic, so the posterior is Gaussian:
\[
  p(\bm f\mid \bm z)
  =
  \Normal(\bm \mu,\bm Q^{-1}).
\]
The precision matrix can be written as
\[
  \bm Q
  =
  \lambda_l\bm L
  +
  \lambda_e\bm K
  +
  \bm A^\top\bm R^{-1}\bm A
  +
  \epsilon\bm I.
\]
Here $\bm L$ is the weighted graph Laplacian, $\bm K$ is the sparse precision
contribution from the ordered copy-number curvature and epistasis penalties,
$\bm A$ selects anchor nodes from the full graph, and $\bm R$ is the diagonal
anchor variance matrix.

The right-hand side is
\[
  \bm b
  =
  \bm A^\top\bm R^{-1}\bm z.
\]
The posterior mean solves
\[
  \bm Q\bm\mu=\bm b,
  \qquad
  \bm \mu=\bm Q^{-1}\bm b.
\]
The marginal posterior variance for node $v$ is
\[
  \Var(f_v\mid \bm z)
  =
  (\bm Q^{-1})_{vv}.
\]

The implementation assembles $\bm Q$ as a sparse matrix and uses RcppEigen
sparse linear algebra to solve for $\bm \mu$ and the diagonal of
$\bm Q^{-1}$.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter Selection}

The global layer has three main hyperparameters:
\[
  \lambda_l,\qquad \lambda_e,\qquad \sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}.
\]
If each grid contains a single value, these are treated as fixed. If multiple
values are supplied, \texttt{alfak2} uses leave-one-anchor-out cross-validation.
For each anchor $a$, the model removes $a$, predicts its fitness from the
remaining anchors, and obtains $\mu_{-a,a}$. The score is
\[
  \operatorname{CV}(\lambda_l,\lambda_e,\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}})
  =
  \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{eval}}|}
  \sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{eval}}}
  \frac{(z_a-\mu_{-a,a})^2}{R_a}.
\]
The selected values are
\[
  (\lambda_l^\star,\lambda_e^\star,\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}^\star)
  =
  \arg\min
  \operatorname{CV}(\lambda_l,\lambda_e,\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}}).
\]

\subsection{Near-Field Extrapolation Methods}

The eight non-baseline methods use the same anchors and the same bounded graph
but restrict reliable reporting to $d(v)\le d_{\max}$. They are intended to
estimate fitness in the vicinity of the observed karyotypes from the local
fitness estimates.

\subsubsection{\texttt{edge\_effect\_empirical\_bayes}}

This method estimates average one-step copy-number fitness effects. Each
directed edge has type
\[
  \tau(e)=(c(e),s(e)),
\]
where $c(e)$ is chromosome index and $s(e)\in\{-1,+1\}$ is loss or gain. For an
anchor-to-anchor edge $e=(u,v)$, define
\[
  r_e=z_v-z_u,\qquad
  V_e=R_u+R_v,\qquad
  \omega_e=\frac{w_e}{V_e}.
\]
The ridge-shrunk empirical Bayes estimate for edge type $\tau$ is
\[
  \hat\delta_{\tau}
  =
  \frac{\sum_{e:\tau(e)=\tau}\omega_e r_e}
       {\sum_{e:\tau(e)=\tau}\omega_e+\lambda_\delta}.
\]
The default code uses \texttt{ridge\_delta = 4}. For a one-hop target $v$,
candidate predictions from direct anchor parents $u$ are
\[
  \hat f_v^{(u)}
  =
  z_u+\hat\delta_{\tau(u,v)}.
\]
For two-hop targets, the method reuses the path-combination machinery but sets
the interaction shrinkage effectively to infinity, so second-order interactions
are shrunk to zero. Candidate paths are combined by inverse variance and path
weight. The final variance includes anchor variance, edge-effect uncertainty,
and between-path dispersion.

The implementation first copies any available local posterior estimates within
$d_{\max}$ into the global summary. Only remaining missing near-field nodes are
filled by the edge-effect predictor. This makes the method conservative: when
TMB already estimated a nearby local node, that local estimate is kept.

\subsubsection{\texttt{edge\_effect\_interaction\_path\_ensemble}}

This method extends the previous model with second-order path interactions.
For a two-edge path
\[
  u\to m\to v,
\]
with edge types $\tau_1$ and $\tau_2$, it fits an interaction correction
\[
  \gamma_{\tau_1,\tau_2}
\]
from anchor triplets. The residual used for training is
\[
  z_v-z_u-\hat\delta_{\tau_1}-\hat\delta_{\tau_2}.
\]
The ridge estimate has the same form as the one-step effect:
\[
  \hat\gamma_{\tau_1,\tau_2}
  =
  \frac{\sum \omega r}
       {\sum \omega+\lambda_\gamma},
\]
with default \texttt{ridge\_gamma = 25}.

For a one-hop target,
\[
  \hat f_v^{(u)}=z_u+\hat\delta_{\tau(u,v)}.
\]
For a two-hop target,
\[
  \hat f_v^{(u,m)}
  =
  z_u+\hat\delta_{\tau(u,m)}
      +\hat\delta_{\tau(m,v)}
      +\hat\gamma_{\tau(u,m),\tau(m,v)}.
\]
All valid paths from directly informed anchors are ensembled. The path weight is
the product of edge weights along the path, and candidate predictions are
combined using inverse variance weighting plus a between-path variance term:
\[
  \widehat{\Var}(f_v)
  =
  \sum_p \alpha_p V_p
  +
  \sum_p \alpha_p(\hat f_{vp}-\hat f_v)^2.
\]

\subsubsection{\texttt{local\_polynomial\_stencil}}

For each target karyotype $x_0$, this method fits a weighted local polynomial
regression to nearby anchors. Let
\[
  h_a=x_a-x_0,\qquad
  d_a=\|x_a-x_0\|_1.
\]
The design matrix contains an intercept, linear terms, and diagonal quadratic
terms:
\[
  X_a =
  \left[
    1,\;
    h_{a1},\ldots,h_{ap},\;
    \frac{1}{2}h_{a1}^2,\ldots,\frac{1}{2}h_{ap}^2
  \right].
\]
If there are enough local training anchors, the implementation also adds a
limited number of pairwise terms $h_{ac}h_{ad}$. The default limit is
\texttt{max\_pairwise\_terms = 20}.

The anchor weights are
\[
  \omega_a
  =
  \frac{
    \exp(-d_a/b)\,q_{\mathrm{tier}(a)}
  }{
    R_a
  },
\]
where $b$ is \texttt{bandwidth} and $q_{\mathrm{tier}}$ downweights weaker
support tiers:
\[
q_{\mathrm{direct}}=1,\qquad
q_{\mathrm{local\ borrowed}}=0.6,\qquad
q_{\mathrm{weakly\ supported}}=0.3.
\]
The coefficient vector is the ridge solution
\[
  \hat\beta
  =
  (X^\top W X+\lambda I_0)^{-1}X^\top Wz,
\]
where the intercept is not penalized. Because the design is centered at
$x_0$, the predicted target fitness is the intercept $\hat\beta_0$. Prediction
variance is estimated from weighted residual variance and the intercept
leverage. If the linear solve fails, the method falls back to a weighted mean.

\subsubsection{\texttt{local\_NNGP\_or\_GPnn}}

This method is a nearest-neighbor Gaussian process on copy-number vectors. For
each prediction target, it selects the $k$ nearest anchors by lattice distance
and anchor variance. The default is \texttt{k = 30}, capped by the number of
available anchors. The kernel is exponential in $L_1$ copy-number distance:
\[
  K(x,y)=\exp\{-\|x-y\|_1/\ell\}.
\]
The local covariance matrix is
\[
  \bm K_{\mathrm{local}}
  =
  K(X,X)+\operatorname{diag}(R_a)+\nu I,
\]
where $\nu$ is the nugget. The prior mean is the inverse-variance weighted
anchor mean. The predictive mean and variance are the standard GP conditional
quantities.

The method tunes $(\ell,\nu)$ by leave-one-anchor-out RMSE on the grid
\[
  \ell\in\{0.75,1.5,3\},\qquad
  \nu\in\{0.02,0.05\}
\]
unless those grids are overridden. If the Cholesky factorization of a local GP
system fails, the implementation falls back to an inverse-variance weighted
kernel mean and records the fallback in diagnostics.

\subsubsection{\texttt{delta\_tree\_ensemble}}

This method learns parent-to-target fitness deltas from tabular features. For
an ordered anchor pair $(u,v)$ with $1\le \|x_v-x_u\|_1\le 2$, the response is
\[
  y_{uv}=z_v-z_u.
\]
The feature vector includes parent fitness mean and standard deviation, parent
support weight, parent count totals, target support distance, path length, edge
weight, parent copy numbers, target copy numbers, signed copy-number deltas,
and absolute copy-number deltas.

The backend currently uses \texttt{xgboost} when it is installed and the
training table is large enough. Otherwise it uses a ridge regression fallback:
\[
  y = \alpha + X\beta + \epsilon,\qquad
  \hat\beta
  =
  (X^\top X+\lambda I_0)^{-1}X^\top y.
\]
The code checks for several tree libraries, but the implemented non-ridge
training path is currently \texttt{xgboost}; other detected backends fall back
to ridge if \texttt{xgboost} is unavailable or fitting fails.

For a target $v$, candidate parent anchors are anchors within
$1,\ldots,d_{\max}$ lattice steps. Each candidate prediction is
\[
  \hat f_v^{(u)}
  =
  z_u+\widehat{\Delta}(u,v).
\]
Candidates are combined by inverse variance and path-distance weighting.
The residual standard deviation of the fitted delta model contributes to the
prediction variance.

\subsubsection{\texttt{tabpfn\_nearfield\_feature\_model}}

This method name is reserved for a TabPFN-style near-field tabular feature
model. The current implementation checks whether \texttt{reticulate} and the
Python module \texttt{tabpfn} are available, records that dependency status,
and then delegates to the same delta-feature pipeline used by
\texttt{delta\_tree\_ensemble}; consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\texttt{tabpfn\_nearfield\_feature\_model}
&\equiv \text{delta-feature model}\\
&\quad \text{with TabPFN dependency diagnostics.}
\end{aligned}
\]
in the current code. When TabPFN is unavailable, diagnostics report
\[
  \texttt{tabpfn\_unavailable\_used\_tree\_fallback}.
\]
This distinction is important for benchmark interpretation: rows for this
method should not be read as evidence for an actual TabPFN model unless
diagnostics show a real TabPFN backend in a future implementation.

\subsubsection{\texttt{kronecker\_or\_graph\_trend\_filtering}}

This method induces the near-field subgraph
\[
  \mathcal{G}_{\le d_{\max}}
  =
  \{v\in\mathcal{G}:d(v)\le d_{\max}\}
\]
and solves a graph trend-filtering problem. In the current implementation the
backend is graph-based, not a Kronecker lattice solver. The objective is an
anchor weighted least-squares term plus first- and second-difference penalties:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \min_{\bm f}\quad
  &
  \frac{1}{2}
  \sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}_{\le d_{\max}}}
  \frac{(f_a-z_a)^2}{R_a}
  +
  \lambda_1\sum_{(i,j)\in E_{\le d_{\max}}}|f_i-f_j|\\
  &+
  \lambda_2
  \sum_{i\to j\to k}
  |f_i-2f_j+f_k|
  +
  \frac{\rho}{2}\|\bm f\|_2^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
The code solves this with an iteratively reweighted quadratic approximation:
\[
  |u|\approx \frac{u^2}{\max(|u^{\mathrm{old}}|,\epsilon)}.
\]
The defaults are \texttt{lambda1 = 0.25}, \texttt{lambda2 = 0.1},
\texttt{max\_iter = 80}, and \texttt{tol = 1e-4}. The reported standard
deviation is a shared residual scale estimated from anchor residuals.

Because this implementation uses dense linear algebra on the induced near-field
graph, it has a safety limit \texttt{max\_dense\_nodes = 1500}. If the induced
graph is larger, the method falls back to the interaction path ensemble and
records \texttt{fallback\_path\_ensemble} in diagnostics.

\subsubsection{\texttt{truncated\_nearfield\_gmrf}}

This method is the graph Gaussian posterior restricted to the near-field
induced subgraph. It builds
\[
  \mathcal{G}_{\le d_{\max}}
\]
and then calls the same sparse Gaussian posterior solver used by
\texttt{graph\_gaussian\_baseline}, but only on that induced graph. It therefore
uses the same anchor observation model, graph Laplacian penalty, curvature
penalty, epistasis penalty, and hyperparameter selection:
\[
  (\lambda_l,\lambda_e,\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}})
  \quad\text{are selected or fixed exactly as in the baseline GMRF.}
\]
After solving, the near-field posterior means and standard deviations are
copied back into the full graph summary; all nodes with $d(v)>d_{\max}$ are
left \texttt{out\_of\_scope}. This method is useful when the goal is accurate
direct and near-neighbor shape preservation without allowing far-field graph
states to influence the near-field posterior.

\section{The Joint-Count Pipeline}
\label{sec:s-joint-count}

\texttt{fit\_alfak2(..., pipeline = "joint\_count")} fits the local count
model of Supplement~\ref{sec:s-local}, the TMB model \texttt{local\_model},
once on the entire bounded target graph rather than on a local graph, with a
structured landscape prior in place of the independent fitness prior. Fitness
is modelled as the sum of a baseline, a main effect and a curvature term for
each chromosome, pairwise chromosome interactions, an independent residual and
edge effects, all Gaussian, with default standard deviations of 0.5,
$0.07/\sqrt p$, $0.04/\sqrt p$, $0.04/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{pairs}}}$, 0.125 and
0.25, respectively. Interaction directions that the observed karyotypes cannot
separate may be assigned a separate prior, and several prior components may be
weighted by their marginal evidence. The forward model is the division
process, refined by step halving until the changes in frequencies and in log
growth fall below a tolerance. In contrast to the sparse joint model, this
pipeline has no balance prior, no domain constructed around abundant clones
and no calibration of uncertainty, and it was not evaluated on the sparse
two-sample benchmark.
